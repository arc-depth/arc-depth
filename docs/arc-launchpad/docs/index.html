What the project does

The floor is not a promise. It is a function.

Every launch has its own vault. Collateral goes in, and there is exactly one way out: by burning tokens. The burn shrinks the supply and the payout shrinks the collateral by the same proportion, so the ratio never moves — somebody leaving does not lower the floor for whoever stays.

01

There is one way out

redeem() is open to everyone and free: payout = collateral × amount ÷ supply. A rescue function, a sweep, an emergency withdraw, a recipient-address parameter — none of them exist. A function like that gets written in good faith, and then it is either used or stolen.

02

The price cannot stay below the floor

If it does, anyone may buy there and redeem at the vault for more. Nobody has to defend the floor; defending it pays. A redemption carries no fee at all, because a fee on the way out would let the price sit that much further below the floor.

03

Liquidity cannot be pulled

The launch's pool position is burned the moment it is born. A removal is refused at the contract level, and there is no owner to do the refusing either: nobody owns the position. A rug pull is technically impossible, not merely promised against.

04

And it cannot be added later

The vault's bytecode contains no DELEGATECALL, CALLCODE or SELFDESTRUCT — not one instruction that could change its code. "We will not add one" is a promise; "we cannot add one" is a measurable property. Every contract on this page is scanned for it separately.

How it works

One transaction. Four things at once.

You bring the collateral; the rest finishes inside a single transaction. No waiting, no operator, nothing to be delivered to you later.

1

Choose the collateral and deposit it

USDC, cirBTC, WETH, EURC — or any other token whose address you paste. There is no allow list. You pick the trade tax that funds the floor too, anywhere from 1% to 5%. The launch fee is a flat 2% of your first buy, whatever the collateral is.

2

The vault fills and the floor is born

All of the collateral goes into this token's own vault. Floor = vault ÷ circulating, where unsold inventory sitting in the pool is not counted as somebody's holding. That number never falls: a redemption shrinks both sides by the same proportion.

3

The pool opens with both sides real

The supply left after your own share goes in as a ladder above the opening price, and your payment, less the fee, is placed below it as an actual bid. An earlier design put only tokens in: the launch reported success and the pool came back holding $0.00 of money, so there was nothing to sell into. Neither side can be withdrawn again.

4

Your own share

Your payment, less the 2% fee, becomes tokens at the opening price and lands in your wallet in the same transaction. It is not a swap, so there is no slippage and the snipe tax has nothing to apply to. It is not free either: at the opening price the whole supply is worth $3,500, and what you receive is worth exactly what you paid net — measured across five payments, to within 0 raw units.

Fees

2% to the protocol, 1% to 5% to the floor.

Two separate things come off a trade and it is worth keeping them apart. The protocol takes 2%, split five ways and fixed in the code. On top of that sits the tax the token's creator chose, 1% to 5%, and that part does not reach the protocol at all — it goes into that token's vault and raises the floor for everyone still holding.

Where the 2% goes

0.70% to pool depth, 0.50% to protocol liquidity, 0.30% to the token's founder, 0.30% to the developer pool and 0.20% to operations. The first two are locked and cannot be withdrawn by anyone, us included. The developer pool pays out only through a vote that has to survive a timelock.

Where the creator's tax goes

Straight into that token's vault, on a buy and on a sell alike, as money. No conversion, no price lookup, no bridge. The collateral grows while the supply does not, so the floor rises for everyone still holding — and a large sale pays an extra slice that rides with the vault's share, not the protocol's.

The first five seconds

At the opening a sniper pays a surcharge of up to 99%, quartering every second and gone after five. It applies to buys only. The founder's own first buy is not exempted by a flag: it is not a swap at all, so there is nothing for the surcharge to apply to.

Whichever route you come through

The hook is part of the pool's identity — keccak(PoolKey) includes the hook address. So the Uniswap interface, an aggregator, a relay or a cross-chain router: all of them pay the same fee. Asking for an exact output used to escape it entirely; that hole is closed, and the fix is readable in the hook's address, which ends in 0xacc.

Of that 2%, the part we can actually spend is 0.20% — operations. Depth and liquidity are locked and nobody can withdraw them, the founder's 0.30% is the token's founder and not us, and the developer pool pays out only through a vote that has to clear a timelock. It is worth stating the small number rather than the big one: a protocol that takes a cut of swaps in its own pools has a reason to want a lot of trading, and that is a reason worth keeping small.

Collateral

Nothing is banned. Nothing is blessed either.

Collateral is not picked from an allow list, it is measured. At launch the collateral's report card is written to the chain once and never changes again — a buyer reads a measurement, not a promise.

?

Why not a list

Maintaining a list needs an owner, and nothing in this project has one. It also cannot be written honestly: "has a USDC pair" is true of 31,787 tokens on Arc. "Top 100" is data the chain cannot see. And "deep pool" can be gamed — liquidity placed for one block is pulled in the next.

!

What the report card says

Decimals, supply, code size; whether it has an owner, whether it can mint, freeze, pause or upgrade. All of it through staticcall — that is, by asking the real code behind the proxy, not by looking at bytecode.

⚠

The symbol is not trusted

There is a live token on Arc whose symbol is USDC and whose name is "Unconditional Support Dog Coin": 18 decimals, a billion supply, 26 USDC pools. A screen that trusted the symbol would describe a launch backed by it as "dollar-backed". The report card looks at the address, not the symbol.

=

The report card is not a verdict

These are facts about a contract, not judgements about an asset. All four of Arc's major assets are upgradeable proxies with an owner and a pause — a screen that painted that red would be saying USDC cannot be trusted. The fact is written down; the reader makes the call.

Guarantees

Structure, not a promise.

None of the following is a commitment. Each one is either a function the contract does not have, an instruction its bytecode does not contain, or a number you can read off the chain.

01

There are no keys

Seven live contracts were searched for withdraw, sweep, rescue, owner, transferOwnership, pause, setFee and upgradeTo. None of those selectors is present in any of them — not set to zero, not guarded: absent.

02

The code cannot change

The bytecode is scanned instruction by instruction for anything that could replace it, and the running code is compared against the compiler's output byte for byte. Both are re-checked on every test run, not once at deployment.

03

Every exit is named

The ways value can leave a vault are a short closed list, and each one has its recipient fixed in the code rather than passed in by whoever calls it. An exit that is not on the list fails the audit even if it looks harmless — the point is that the list cannot grow.

04

The floor is written from measurement

The floor price is computed on a 1e36 scale. It used to be 1e18, which rounded six-decimal collateral down to 1 — every significant digit gone. A test caught that, not a user.

FAQ

Honest answers.

Can you withdraw the money?

No, and that is not a promise: the vault has no withdraw function. With no function there is nothing to call, and adding one by changing the code is impossible because the vault carries no instruction that could change its code.

Can the token go to zero?

If the collateral goes to zero, yes. The floor is worth what the collateral is worth: a launch backed by USDC has a floor in dollars, one backed by cirBTC has a floor in BTC, and if BTC falls the floor falls with it. What is guaranteed is that the ratio does not break, not the price of the collateral.

Can the founder sell everything and run?

They can. Their tokens are theirs from the first transaction — there is no vesting in a launch, and saying otherwise would be inventing a lock that does not exist. What they cannot take back is the money they put into the pool, and their selling does not lower the floor: the collateral is already in the vault, and the tax on that sale goes into it too.

Has it been audited?

Not externally audited. 806 automated checks run against the real Arc chain, and the defects they found are written into the code as comments — what was wrong and why. That does not take the place of an audit report.

Can I avoid the fee through an aggregator?

No. The hook is part of the pool's identity, so whichever interface you come through, afterSwap runs. Escaping by fixing the output side (exact-output) is closed too — it was open for a while, it was measured, and it was closed.

Is this investment advice?

It is not. This is a meme coin, and having a floor does not make it a good investment — it only makes the location of zero something you can know.
